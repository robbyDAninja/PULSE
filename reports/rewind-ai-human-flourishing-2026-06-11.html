When collection started, the conversation already had its whole script. Universal basic income as the answer — a review of 122 experiments made the rounds. AI-proofing as a personal duty, per the WSJ. New York launched a commission to study the workforce impact. And AI executives supplied the menace: per Newsweek's headline, only two types of people would survive the job market. Everyone was bracing. Nobody had the data yet.

April wks 3–6

The numbers land — and the labs propose taxing themselves.

One April week carried the quarter's heaviest freight. A Goldman Sachs report dominated the headlines — 16,000 U.S. jobs a month, a "scarring effect," yearslong pay cuts for the displaced (those are the headlines' claims; the corpus stores what they assert). The same week, OpenAI floated robot taxes, a public wealth fund, and a four-day workweek. And one quiet headline undercut both: the dataset that could actually predict AI job displacement barely exists. The loudest claims and the thinnest data shared a single news cycle.

03
May wks 7–10

The prophets recant — while the first real protections ship.

Then the strangest turn in the corpus: the people who wrote the apocalypse began withdrawing it. Sam Altman fell out of love with UBI — the topic's biggest Hacker News moment to that point — and by month's end Time had him saying the jobs apocalypse he once predicted probably won't happen. (The corpus keeps the cynical read too: Fortune noted both Altman and Amodei walking back their prophecies "as they eye blockbuster IPOs.") Meanwhile, protection stopped being theoretical: Chinese courts ruled AI-driven layoffs illegal, and Newsom signed a first-of-its-kind executive order for displaced workers.

04
June wks 11–13

The argument changes subject: from jobs to what makes us human.

With the apocalypse postponed, the fight moved to meaning. The Times argued writing is fundamental to how we think — a case against outsourcing cognition itself. Martin Scorsese called AI "creatively freeing" and was rebuked by working artists and their union within the week. And the single loudest signal in this topic's thirteen weeks — 204 points — wasn't a layoff number. It was Rich Sutton, a Turing Award winner, on AI creativity and discovery. The displacement story commanded the headlines; the meaning story commanded the attention.

The Thread
In one quarter, the prophets recanted, the protections shipped — and the loudest signal was about creativity, not job counts.

The keywords were tuned for anxiety. What they caught was a renegotiation.

Why this matters — if you run a business

"Will AI take the jobs?" was March's question. June asked a better one: what is the work for?

The quarter's honest ledger: the scariest numbers arrived alongside an admission that the predictive data barely exists; the loudest prophet withdrew his own prophecy; courts and a governor built the first guardrails for workers. What held attention by June wasn't displacement — it was agency: what people decide, judge, and make. That's the practical read for a small business. The question isn't whether AI replaces your team; it's whether you redesign their work around the things this whole quarter argued humans keep. Grounded hope, with receipts.
